# The pond machine, inventoried as a builder needs it

_2026-08-11. Scope: the small raft that demonstrably ran — the one the archive files as "the 2016
pond machine." Nothing here describes the container machine, the drum raft, or the 2024 CAD design.
Every observation is tied to a named file and a named region. Numbers handwritten by the inventor
are reported as his assertions and never as fact. The block of performance claims on
`DX5b+notes1.jpg` is quarantined and is not repeated anywhere in this document._

---

## The answer in one paragraph

The pond machine's photographic record is **three still photographs and one 26.6-second video, all
made by the inventor, none of them a measurement**. That is thinner than the archive believed in one
respect and thicker in two others: the video is *not* an independent second angle (it looks at the
machine from broadly the same bearing as the best photograph), but two further photographs exist that
no project document has used — one of which is a near-broadside view of the whole machine floating on
open water, and one of which is the sharpest view of the deck hardware in the entire corpus. From
those four records a builder can reconstruct the machine's **topology** with confidence — hull count,
rotor type and blade count, mast and stay arrangement, what sits on the deck and roughly how it is
plumbed — and can reconstruct **almost none of its dimensions**. No object of known size touches the
machine in any frame; no pipe stamp is legible; no component label is readable; there is no drawing,
no bill of materials, no cut list, and no assembly note. The single exception is the open-water
photograph, which contains three Canada geese floating on the same water plane and is therefore the
only route to absolute scale that exists — a route nobody has taken, and one that a careful hour of
rectification would close.

## 1. The complete evidence inventory, corrected

Four primary records. Everything else that mentions this machine is derived from them.

**Record B — `Personal/Seasteading/_ARCHIVED/06_Media/Videos/Robot in pond close up.mp4`** ·

**Record C — `~/Downloads/Model-JTfree1NEW/lawsofmechcology1.jpg`** · 2560 × 1920 · same SPH‑M840
phone · EXIF DateTimeOriginal **2016-02-25 10:46:04**. **The most valuable and least used image in the
record.** Near-broadside, low angle, from across the water. The machine is **out on open water, well
clear of the bank, floating with real freeboard**, with three Canada geese on the surface beside it.
A block of the inventor's "Laws of Mechcology" text is overlaid on the lower half; the machine itself
is unobstructed.

**Record D — `~/Downloads/Model-JTfree1NEW/BioMass#.jpg`** · 1675 × 871 as saved, but its EXIF records
an original width of 4608 px — this is a **crop of a larger frame**. Samsung ST66/ST68 compact (the
same camera whose clock is independently known to be wrong on the container-machine photographs; this
file reads 2012-03-07 **02:20 AM** for a daylight shot, so its date is not usable). The left third of
the graphic is a **close, low-angle photograph of the deck** — the sharpest view of the masthead
housing, the electronics box, the cylindrical power component and the red-lidded tub anywhere in the
corpus.

### Three corrections this forces

**The machine is not a 2016 build.** Record A was taken **30 December 2015**. Its 2016-02-21 file
date is when the annotated version was saved. Calling this "the 2016 pond machine" is off by a year
at the point where it matters most — it sits it *after* the container machine's April–July 2015
build rather than overlapping it. The honest label is **the pond machine, photographed December 2015
to February 2016**.

**The video is not a second angle.** Every asymmetric feature agrees between Record A and Record B —
the electronics box on the far side of the deck, the pump to the right of the red tub, the solar panel
at the same end, the standpipe at the same corner, the bank and reeds to the right. The video is taken
from a somewhat lower elevation and in sun rather than overcast, which is worth having, but it does
not triangulate. **Record C is the second angle**, and it is roughly perpendicular to A and B.

**Record D's original may still exist.** The saved graphic is a downsample of a 4608 × 3456 camera
frame. If that frame survives on the inventor's machine it would roughly triple the linear resolution
on the deck hardware. That is the cheapest single upgrade available to this whole lane.

## 2. What is actually on the machine, counted

### Hulls

**Two.** A catamaran. Both are large-diameter white plastic tube, closed at each end with a
shallow-domed cap seated behind a visible shoulder — the same "shoulder formed by end caps and
couplings" the inventor describes in his own model annotation for a later design. Each hull carries at
least one visible circumferential coupling band along its length, so each is assembled from more than
one length of tube. No through-fittings, drains, vents or inspection ports are visible on either hull
in any record.

Diameter ratio, hull to frame pipe, measured off Record A where the two run in contact: roughly
**2:1 to 3:1**. The spread is real — the two tubes sit at different distances from the camera at every
point where both are visible, and no perpendicular section can be measured cleanly. Absolute diameters
are unknown.

### Frame

A **ladder of smaller white PVC pipe laid on top of the two hulls**, with a rail along each hull and
transverse members between and beyond them. The count of transverse members cannot be pinned exactly:
in every record at least one cross member, one mast stay and one lashed cable run overlap in
projection at the points where the count would be decided. A defensible reading is **a bow member, a
stern member, and three or four intermediate members including the one the mast steps on** — treat
that as a range, not a number.

What *is* unambiguous about the frame:

Standard PVC socket fittings throughout — tees, at least one cross, and at the aft corner a **stepped
corner built from a pair of elbows and a short jog piece** rather than a single fitting. Visible in
`reference-clean/pond-machine-2016-noclaims.jpg` around the solar panel's inboard corner.

**Transverse members overhang the rails as open, uncapped stubs**, projecting outboard past the hulls
by a substantial fraction of the beam. At least four such stubs are visible; the bore of the cut end
is legible on three of them. They are open to the sky and sit close to the waterline.

**Cable ties do structural work.** White nylon ties with long uncut tails appear at most joints,
wrapped around the fitting or binding rail to hull. Whether the socket joints beneath them are
solvent-welded cannot be determined — no glue fillet or primer stain is visible anywhere.

**Four vertical open-topped standpipes** pass down through tees in the frame and continue below the
waterline. Two are directly legible (far corner and far-right corner, Record A); a third is at the
left edge of the uncropped original. Their tops are cut square and left open. Their function is
nowhere stated and cannot be inferred — candidates are pump suction, discharge return, or a
stabilising skeg, and the record does not choose between them.

### Mast and stays

A **single white PVC mast** stepped at a fitting on the frame, plus **four rigid metal stays** running
from a bracket just below the masthead housing down to four widely-spaced points on the frame. The
stays are grey galvanised thin-wall tube — the cut ends are open and rusting internally, visible in
`DX5b+notes1.jpg` where two of them overhang a rail. At the masthead end each is **flattened and
drilled** and bolted to a tab; at the frame end each simply overhangs and is held by a **white plastic
saddle clamp**. No turnbuckles, no cables, no tensioning hardware — this is a rigid tripod-style stay
set, not a rigged mast.

A relevant note from the inventor's own hand, on a *different* drawing: `SF-1m5x3c.jpg` (the JT‑1
concept) carries the annotation *"make a prototype using 2"pvc pipe for the frame & 1" EMT"*, and
`SF-1m5x3b.jpg` carries *"use 2x2 square tube & hubs, with 1-1/2" EMT to make the frames."* The pond
machine is built exactly to that recipe — PVC frame, EMT stays. **This is the only place in the entire
archive where a pipe size and a stay material are named together, and it is not on this machine's
drawing.** It is a lead worth chasing with the inventor, not a dimension to publish.

### Wind rotor

**Vertical-axis, five blades, two spider hubs.** The blade count reads five in all three still
photographs and in the video. The confirming detail is the hub: at each level four white radial arms
are visible ending in U-shaped feet that clamp a blade's inner edge, plus a **fifth arm foreshortened
toward the camera, visible only as a horizontal strap laid across the near blade**. Four arms plus one
strap equals five attachments per hub.

The blades are **rolled sheet metal, not flat plate** — the leading edge shows a curled lip at the top
corner, and the lit face carries a specular highlight that walks across the curvature. Bright
mill-finish, consistent with aluminium. Rotor swept width and blade height are roughly equal (about
1:1 in Record A's projection).

**The rotor was turning during the video.** Frame-to-frame comparison across a static-camera stretch
shows the blade pattern changing continuously. That is the single strongest piece of "it ran"
evidence in the record — stronger than the still photographs, which show only a machine sitting in
water.

Below the rotor: a white PVC cylindrical housing built from a coupling and a cap, with a **bolted top
plate** the rotor shaft passes through, and a **cream-coloured collar** between plate and housing. On
the shaft between the two spiders sits a dark, irregular component — unidentified; it reads as a
coupling or a slip-ring assembly. The housing is mounted on a **PVC tee or wye at the top of the
mast** (clearest in Record D). Whatever generator is inside has never been photographed, described or
named.

### Solar panel

One panel, lying **flat on the frame** and, in Records A and B, **partly submerged**. It is not a
crystalline module: black moulded plastic frame with **radiused corners**, a raised lip all round,
**corner mounting holes**, and a laminate showing no cell squares and no busbar grid.

The strong finding: **the same panel type — plausibly the same physical panel — appears photographed
face-on and fully lit in `photoschematicAssembly1b.jpg`, the 2022 wiring photo-schematic, where its
brand badge is legible.** Enlarged and mirrored, the badge reads **Schumacher**, with the maker's red
swoosh and "ELECTRIC" beneath. The laminate there shows the fine horizontal stripe lines of an
**amorphous thin-film** cell, and the frame, radiused corners, corner holes and badge position all
match the pond machine's panel. Schumacher's product in this shape is a 12 V amorphous battery
maintainer; the model number is not readable and should be confirmed against a catalogue rather than
taken from this document.

The inventor's red arrow on Record A reads **"15 watt solar panel."** That is his assertion. It is
consistent with the class of hardware identified but is not corroborated by anything.

### Pump and plumbing

A **small 12 V diaphragm pump** sits at deck centre beside the red-lidded tub: a black moulded pump
head with four fasteners across its face, coupled to a green-and-white ribbed cylindrical body, with
**tan flexible tubing** looping away to the right and back.

The archive contains a **fully legible pump label** — again in the 2022 photo-schematic, and again
never transcribed. Enlarged, it reads: model **PLD-1206** · rated voltage **12 V (9–14.4 V)** · rated
power **48 W** · maximum pressure **1 MPa** · largest flow **4 L/min**. This is a commodity Chinese
diaphragm pump. **It is a 2022 document and cannot be assumed to be the pond machine's unit**, but it
is the same class of hardware, from the same builder, and it is the only pump in the corpus with a
readable nameplate.

That matters arithmetically. The inventor's arrow on Record A reads **"1/10th HP pump"** — 74.6 W. A
PLD‑1206 is 48 W. If the pond pump is of that class, the annotation overstates it by roughly half.
(For the record, `reference-clean/README.md` already settled the transcription dispute — the caption
reads "1/10th HP," not the "1/100 HP" of `due-diligence/systems/energy.md:69` or the "[1/4] hp" of
`due-diligence/systems/frame.md:40`. I read it independently at high magnification and confirm
"1/10th HP pump.")

The wet path, as far as it can be traced:

A large-diameter **grey flexible hose** forms a wide loop from the pump area, down around and beneath
the clear jar, and back up to a **white barbed plastic union**, which connects to a rigid tube running
outboard and lashed to the frame with a black tie. Where that tube terminates is not visible.

A **clear plastic jar** with a wide circular mouth lies on the deck at the waterline, its embossed
brand panel facing the camera and **illegible at any magnification** (roughly nine characters in a
recessed rectangle). A **white waxy or flocculent deposit** is visible inside it.

A **round translucent tub with a red snap lid** sits at deck centre. The lid has a moulded finger-pull
and carries torn paper labels. Through the tub wall a **dark blue rectangular object** occupies most of
the lower half. Its edges are straight and unwrapped where the tub is round, so it is inside the tub
rather than printed on it. It is the right shape and colour for a small sealed lead-acid battery in a
blue case — and equally the right shape for a blue-labelled item or a foam block. **The record does not
identify it, and this is the closest thing to a battery anywhere on the machine.**

There is **no nozzle**, no pressure vessel, no filter housing, no valve and no gauge visible anywhere
on this machine, in any record. Nothing that resembles the kinetic-nozzle hardware belongs to it.

### Electrical

**A cylindrical two-terminal component** lies on the deck beside the electronics box, on standoff
feet, with red and black leads. It is the shape and size of a **car-audio stiffening capacitor**.
Supporting this: the 2022 photo-schematic photographs one explicitly — a **Bullz Audio 2.2 farad,
20 VDC low-ESR "digital power capacitor"** — and the SketchUp corpus separately carries an
electrolytic-capacitor component. A farad-class capacitor rather than a battery would explain why no
battery box appears on the machine, and would make the blue object in the tub something else. Both
readings remain open.

**The enclosure is a household plastic parts organiser** — clear-amber, compartment grid visible
through the lid (roughly five columns by three rows), a snap lid, lying flat on a frame rail. No
gasket, no gland, no strain relief.

**Wiring is loose throughout.** Black and red conductors run unsupported between the box, the
capacitor and the pump; several lie in the water. One small brass-coloured item near the box may be a
fuse holder or a crimp connector — not resolvable. There is **no visible fuse, no charge controller,
no switch, no disconnect and no conduit**.

### The energy budget does not close without storage

Taking the inventor's own part labels at face value — 15 W of solar and 15 W of wind, so 30 W of
nameplate generation — and the identified pump class at 48 W:

At the site's latitude and in the last days of December, a **flat, horizontal, partly submerged**
15 W amorphous panel would deliver on the order of **20–35 Wh per day** (roughly 2 peak-sun-hours,
derated for horizontal mounting, wetting and thin-film performance). A 48 W-class pump running on that
alone would run **on the order of 25–45 minutes a day**. A small vertical-axis rotor of this size adds
little in the light winds visible on the water.

That arithmetic is order-of-magnitude and rests on stated assumptions, but its conclusion is robust:
**the machine cannot have run its pump continuously, and must have stored energy and cycled it.**
Nothing in the record describes how — no controller, no timer, no float switch, no duty-cycle note.
This is the largest single hole in the electrical picture, and it is the one a builder hits first.

### What is not on the machine

No propulsion of any kind — no thruster, no bilge-pump cartridge, no motor, no rudder, no skeg. In
Record C the machine sits on open water with no visible tether. The concept drawing
`manatee-manual/ref/DX-5dwPOND.jpg` says the processor "moves around the pond"; the built machine has
nothing to move it with.

No drum. No reactor. No canopy. No second panel. No instrumentation of any kind — no meter, no gauge,
no sensor, no logger. Nothing on this machine measured anything.

## 3. Scale: the one thing missing, and the one way to get it

**No absolute dimension can be read off any record.** No pipe stamp is legible (checked at 14×
magnification on the two clearest markings — both are smudges). No component label is readable on the
machine itself. No person, tool, tape or standard object appears in contact with it in any frame.

Two things must not be used to fill the gap:

`manatee-manual/ref/DX-5dwPOND.jpg` carries dimensions — 6 ft 7‑5/16 in, 11 ft 3‑11/16 in, a pond
diameter of 15 ft 2‑3/16 in, and a rotor dimension whose leading character is obscured by a blade and
therefore cannot be transcribed at all. **That file is a SketchUp rendering of a different machine** —
twin torpedo floats, a glass pyramid canopy, a honeycomb-textured drum, a composited human figure —
and its numbers describe that concept, not this raft. `small-machine-bom-draft-20260807.md` §1 already
says so and is right.

The 2024 CAD's numbers describe a machine that was never built.

**What can be done, and has not been.** `lawsofmechcology1.jpg` puts three Canada geese and the raft
on the same water plane, in the same frame, from a camera whose focal length is recorded in EXIF
(3.55 mm on an SPH‑M840). For any object resting on a plane, apparent size scales with distance below
the horizon, so the raft-to-goose length ratio is recoverable once the horizon row is fixed — and the
horizon can be fixed independently from the road, guardrail and vehicles on the far bank.

A first pass, done by eye rather than by rectification, puts the raft's overall length at roughly
**2.5 to 4 metres (8 to 13 feet)**, with the result strongly sensitive to where the horizon is placed
and to which of the two hulls' ends are being measured. An independent cross-check against the red
tub's diameter — assuming a common 15–25 cm household tub — lands in the same band. What the estimate
establishes firmly is only the **order of magnitude: this is a person-sized raft, not a bench model.**
Do not publish a number from it.

Doing it properly is perhaps an hour of work: pick the goose waterlines and the hull ends carefully,
fix the horizon from the far-bank vehicles, propagate the error bars, and state the result with them.
It would give this project its first real dimension and would let every ratio already measurable in
the photographs — beam to length, panel to hull, mast height to length — convert into actual sizes.
**This is the highest-value unexploited measurement in the whole pond-machine record.**

One ratio is already usable and is scale-free: in Record C, **the height from waterline to rotor top
is about 0.37 of the hull length**.

## 4. Flotation: the machine is shown both awash and floating properly

In Records A and B, at the bank, the machine is **awash** — water covers the deck bays, the red tub is
immersed to mid-height, the solar panel is half under, and the hulls are more than half submerged.

In Record C, two months later and out on open water, the same machine floats with **clear freeboard**:
the hulls show substantial topsides, the frame is dry, and the deck bays are above the surface.

Because the properly-floating photograph is the *later* one, progressive flooding of the hulls is not
the explanation. Candidates that remain: the machine is partly aground in the shallows at the bank, or
it is being held down by the submerged vegetation it is sitting in, or it was loaded differently.
**The record does not settle it, and it matters** — the awash photograph is the one the project has
been treating as the hero image, and it is the one that makes the machine look marginal. Record C is
the photograph that shows it floating as designed.

Related: there is no stability information of any kind. No inclining test, no freeboard measurement,
no displacement figure, no note of where the ballast or the payload sat.

## 5. What a builder could and could not build from this

### Could reconstruct with confidence

The layout: two capped tube hulls, a PVC ladder frame laid across them, cross members overhanging as
open stubs, a central mast on four rigid EMT stays to the frame, a five-blade vertical-axis rotor on a
PVC generator housing at the masthead, one flat amorphous panel lashed to the frame with cable ties,
one small 12 V diaphragm pump at deck centre, one lidded tub and one open jar as the whole wet stage,
one plastic parts box and one farad-class capacitor as the whole electrical installation.

The construction method: standard PVC socket fittings, cable ties as reinforcement and as the
hull-to-frame binding, flattened-and-drilled EMT stay ends bolted at the masthead and clamped with
plastic saddles at the frame, nothing marine-rated anywhere.

The proportions: hull-to-frame diameter ratio, rotor aspect, mast height as a fraction of hull length,
where each component sits relative to the others.

### Could not reconstruct at all

Every dimension. Pipe diameters, wall schedules, cut lengths, hull length, beam, panel size, mast
height, rotor diameter, blade chord — none of them, from any record, without the rectification pass
described above.

The generator. Nothing in the archive names, describes or photographs whatever is inside the masthead
housing. Whether it is a DC motor run backwards, a stepper, a car alternator or a purpose-built
axial-flux unit is unknown, and so is its output, its rectification and its cut-in speed.

The rotor's fabrication. Blade material, thickness, chord, camber and how the arms were made and
fastened. The arms are white and appear moulded or cut from sheet — the record does not say which.

The wiring. There is no circuit diagram for this machine. What connects to what, what fusing exists,
whether the capacitor is across the source or the load, how the pump is switched — all unknown. (The
2022 `photoschematicAssembly1b.jpg` *is* a circuit, complete with photographed parts, solid-state
relays, a bus block, an LCD meter and a perfboard controller — but it is six years later and belongs
to a different machine.)

The process. What the pump drew from, what it discharged into, what the jar collected and how, and
what the four standpipes were for. There is no flow path in the record — only a hose loop, a union
and two containers.

Whether the joints are glued. This determines whether the frame is a permanent assembly or a dry-fit
one, and it is not visible in any frame.

## 6. The cheapest ways to close the gaps, in order of value

1. **Rectify `lawsofmechcology1.jpg` against the geese and the far-bank horizon.** An hour of work.
   Yields the first absolute dimension this machine has ever had, and converts every measurable ratio
   into real sizes.
2. **Find the camera-original of `BioMass#.jpg`.** Its EXIF says the source frame was 4608 px wide;
   the saved graphic is 1675. Triples the linear resolution on the deck hardware, and that crop is
   already the sharpest deck view in the corpus.
3. **Transcribe `photoschematicAssembly1b.jpg` in full.** It is 7332 × 3064, every part is a
   photograph of a real component, and several labels are fully legible at native resolution — the
   PLD‑1206 pump and the Bullz Audio capacitor were read for this document in minutes. Nobody has
   inventoried it. It is a later machine, so it must be labelled as such, but it is by far the richest
   electrical source in the archive.
4. **Ask the inventor five questions**, each of which closes something nothing else can: what pipe
   size is the frame and what size are the hulls; what is inside the masthead housing; was there a
   battery, a capacitor or neither; what are the four vertical standpipes for; and are the frame
   joints glued.
5. **Look for the video's camera-original.** The best copy on disk is 640 × 360 and is itself at least
   one export removed from source (`pond-video-source-20260807.md`). A camera-original would sharpen
   the rotor and deck considerably.

## 7. Confidence

Two hulls, catamaran, capped both ends — HIGH. Visible in all four records.

Vertical-axis rotor — HIGH. Visible in all four records, and the blades are curved sheet, not flat.

Five blades — MODERATE-HIGH. Reads five in all four records; the four-arms-plus-one-foreshortened-strap
count at each hub is the confirming detail. Four is the only plausible alternative.

Rotor turning in the video — HIGH. Frame differencing over a static-camera stretch.

Four rigid EMT stays, flattened and drilled at the masthead, saddle-clamped at the frame — HIGH.

Four open-topped standpipes through the frame into the water — MODERATE-HIGH. Three directly legible;
purpose UNKNOWN.

Panel is an amorphous Schumacher maintainer, or the same product family — MODERATE. The badge is
legible in the photo-schematic and the housing matches the pond machine's panel in frame profile,
corner radius and mounting holes; the two photographs are seven years apart.

Pump is a PLD‑1206-class 12 V diaphragm unit — MODERATE. Same class and same builder, but the
labelled example is a 2022 document.

Blue object inside the red-lidded tub — LOW. It is inside rather than printed on, and that is all that
can be said.

The capacitor reading of the deck cylinder — MODERATE. Shape, standoffs and two terminals fit; the
corpus has a labelled farad capacitor and a CAD capacitor component; no label is readable on the
machine.

Record A is 2015-12-30 and Record C is 2016-02-25 — HIGH. EXIF DateTimeOriginal, same phone, both
internally consistent with the scenes.

Record B is not an independent angle — HIGH. Every asymmetric feature agrees with Record A.

Overall length 2.5–4 m — LOW. Order of magnitude only. Do not publish a number.

Absolute dimensions of anything else — UNKNOWN, and stated plainly: they are not in this archive.
